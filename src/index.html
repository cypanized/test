Choose your knot

Three knots cover almost everything

Start with the Four-in-Hand. Reach for a Half Windsor when you want something more balanced, and save the full Windsor for formal days and wide collars.

Before you start

Thirty seconds of set-up

  1. Button up, collar up

    Fasten the top button and flip the collar up, so the tie can sit right against the collar band.

  2. Seam in, wide end right

    The seam on the back of the tie faces your shirt. Hang the wide end on your right. Left-handed? Mirror every left and right.

  3. Face a mirror

    The guide is drawn as you see yourself in a mirror. Tying someone else’s tie? Switch to Facing you.

  4. Know your finish line

    The finished tip should just touch the top of your belt buckle. Your starting length is what controls this.

Troubleshooting

Fixes for the usual problems

The tie ends up too short

Untie it and start again with the narrow end higher, so the wide end hangs longer. The tie’s length is fixed, so every centimetre you take from the narrow end goes to the wide end. Or switch to a smaller knot: the Four-in-Hand uses the least length.

The tie ends up too long

Start with the narrow end lower. If it’s still long with the narrow end hanging low, try a bigger knot (Half Windsor or Windsor), which uses up more length.

The narrow end hangs below the wide end

You started with the wide end too short. Drop it lower before you begin. Afterwards, tuck the narrow end through the keeper loop on the back of the wide blade.

My knot is lopsided

A Four-in-Hand is naturally a little asymmetric; that’s part of its charm. For a perfectly symmetrical knot, tie a Half Windsor or a Windsor. Either way, keep the narrow end pulled straight down while you slide the knot up.

My dimple keeps disappearing

Make the dimple before the final tighten. Pinch it in place and keep pinching while you slide the knot up. Thicker silks hold a dimple best.

The knot looks too big or too small

Pick the knot to suit the collar and the tie. A chunky tie or a narrow collar? Go smaller (Four-in-Hand). A thin silk tie or a wide spread collar? Go bigger (Half Windsor or Windsor).

I’m left-handed

Start with the wide end on your left and swap every “left” and “right” in the steps. The knots come out as mirror images and look just as good.

How do I untie it without ruining the tie?

Reverse the steps rather than yanking the narrow end through. Silk creases when it’s pulled hard while tight. Hang the tie up or roll it loosely overnight and the creases relax.

The maths of tie knots

Every knot is a short walk

In 1999 the Cambridge physicists Thomas Fink and Yong Mao noticed that tying a tie is a short walk. The wide end only ever moves between three regions: your Left, your Right, and the Centre, up into the neck loop. Each move passes either over the front of the knot (i) or underneath it (o), and every knot ends with T, the tuck through the front.

Under their rules for a good-looking knot they counted 85 ways to tie a tie. In 2015 another team loosened the rules to allow fancier finishes and counted 177,147.

The map beside each step in the guide traces that walk, drawn as you’d see it in a mirror, the same view Fink and Mao used.

Move sequences follow Thomas Fink & Yong Mao, The 85 Ways to Tie a Tie (1999), and the Wikipedia articles on the Four-in-Hand, Half Windsor and Windsor knots. The larger count is from Hirsch et al., “More ties than we thought” (2015).
