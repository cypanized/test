Know the cloth before you fold it

A tie has two ends and two sides. The diagrams above use the same names and the same colours.

BladeThe wide end. It does all the moving and ends up in front.
TailThe narrow end. Hold it still while you tie. It finishes hidden behind the blade.
Seam sideThe back of the tie, drawn in the darker colour. It should never show on a finished knot.
Keeper loopThe small loop on the back of the blade. Slip the tail through it when you are done.
Standard sizeAbout 147 cm (58 in) long and 8 cm (3¼ in) wide. If you are over 190 cm (6 ft 3 in) tall, look for a long tie of about 160 cm (63 in).

The finish: length and dimple

Two details separate a tied tie from a well-tied one.

The tip touches the buckle

The point of the blade should land on your belt buckle. A knot uses the same amount of tie every time, so the fix is exact: if the tip ends 5 cm too high, untie it and start with the wide end 5 cm lower.

Set the dimple while you tighten

The dimple is the small hollow under the knot. Press a fingertip into the middle of the blade just below the knot, pinch the edges around it, and keep the pinch while you slide the knot up.

Tuck the tail

Slip the tail through the keeper loop so it stays behind the blade.

Match the knot to the collar

The knot should fill the gap between the collar points without pushing them apart. Wide collars want wide knots. Thick ties tie bigger, so pair them with smaller knots.

When it goes wrong

The tail hangs below the bladeThe wide end started too high. Untie and start with it lower by the amount the tail overshoots.
The blade stops above the beltSame cause. Lower the wide end at the start by the missing length, or switch to a smaller knot.
The knot leans to one sideA Four-in-Hand leans on purpose. If you want it square, use a Half Windsor or a Pratt.
The seam shows on the frontThe first cross went the wrong way. Check whether your knot starts over or under, and with the seam in or out.
There is a gap under the collarHold the tail and push the knot up until it covers the top button. The button must be done up.
The knot looks bulky or strangledToo many wraps for the cloth. Thick wool or knit ties suit an Oriental or Four-in-Hand. Keep the Windsor for thin silk.

Every knot is a short code

In 1999 two Cambridge physicists, Thomas Fink and Yong Mao, showed that a tie knot is a walk between three regions. The two ends of the tie split the space in front of you into left (L), right (R) and centre (C), the inside of the neck loop. Each move carries the wide end into a different region, heading in toward your shirt or out toward the mirror. The walk ends with T, a tuck through the front band.

They counted 85 knots that a normal tie can make in nine moves or fewer. The diagrams on this page are folded by a small program straight from that code. Drawn as a route around the three regions, each knot has its own fingerprint.
